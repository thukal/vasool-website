A TASMAC empty-carton tender grants the right to collect empty IMFL, IFL, beer and wine cartons and paper waste from retail vending shops — awarded district by district through the Tamil Nadu eProcurement portal. Win one and you have a few hundred shops, a vehicle, two or three men, and an obligation to turn up at every one of them on an agreed cadence.

At the end of the week a number arrives at the office. Four thousand one hundred boxes.

Where did that number come from? Someone counted, somewhere, and remembered. Usually the same someone who drove the van.

## Most of these tenders are funded by more than one person

That is the part the software question turns on. A district tender is frequently run by a small syndicate — one partner puts in capital, another has the vehicles, a third knows the officials. One of them, or somebody they employ, actually runs the route.

The partners who are not on the route have no way to check anything. They see a weekly total and a bank balance. Every disagreement that eventually happens in these arrangements traces back to the same gap: **the only record of what was picked up is the word of the person who picked it up.**

> Partners rarely fall out over the money. They fall out over not being able to check the money.

## Two promises, and only one of them is about boxes

A carton tender actually commits you to two separate things.

**The frequency.** Every shop on the list, once in so many days. That is not a scheduling preference — it is the thing you are being paid for and the thing you will be measured against if anyone complains.

**The count.** What came back, from where.

Most people start with a spreadsheet and solve the second one. The first one quietly breaks, because a spreadsheet organises a round by area — all the shops in one town together — and that mixes a shop due every two days into the same trip as one due weekly. The frequency slips by a day here and three days there, nobody notices for a month, and there is no record to reconstruct it from afterwards.

So the route has to be built on the cadence, not the map. A three-day route contains the shops due every three days in that area, and the list of what is due today falls out of it automatically rather than being worked out each morning from a frequency column.

## Zero is an answer

A shop is shut. A shop has nothing ready. The team arrived, found nothing, and drove on.

If the only thing the system stores is a quantity, that stop is indistinguishable from a stop that never happened. Both are blank.

A visit with nothing collected has to be recorded as a visit — the stop exists, the count is zero, a note explains why. It is a small decision that turns out to carry the whole thing, because the question a partner or a client actually asks weeks later is not "how many boxes" but **"did your team go on Tuesday?"** You can only answer that if "we went and there was nothing" and "we didn't go" are different rows.

## Letting partners look without letting them touch

Here is the arrangement that resolves the trust problem without anybody having to be trusted.

Box collection in Vasool has four separate permission areas: the shops, the routes, the daily team assignments, and the reports. They are granted independently of each other.

That means a partner can be given **the reports and nothing else**. They can open the box summary, the shop-wise totals, route performance and the monthly overview, whenever they like, without asking anyone. And they cannot edit a shop, move a route, reassign a team, or record a visit — not because they have been asked not to, but because that access was never granted to them.

The distinction matters. Showing a partner a figure is a courtesy you can withdraw. Giving them standing access to the record is a structural answer to a structural problem.

Alongside it: staff location during the round, shop pins that get re-verified on site with the date of the last check kept, and a visit history that includes the empty stops. Together those turn "trust me" into "look for yourself".

## This is not really about cartons

The same shape appears wherever an obligation is a **visit** rather than an amount owed: scrap and recyclables rounds, equipment servicing contracts, vending and refill routes, facility checks. The business is paid to turn up on a cadence and to report honestly what happened at each stop, and the party paying is rarely present.

Collection software built for lending assumes money is owed and counts down a balance. None of that applies here. What you need is a list of locations, a frequency that is structurally enforced, a record that survives an empty stop, and a way for people who are not on the route to see all of it.
