Search for the **best app for auto finance tracking** and you get two piles of results that have nothing to do with each other. One pile is personal budgeting apps for someone watching their own car EMIs come down. The other is lending software for the company that gave out the loan.

If you finance vehicles — two-wheelers, three-wheelers, cars, commercial vehicles — you are in the second group, and almost none of the "top 10" lists are written for you. This guide sets out what we would check, in the order we would check it, before putting a vehicle finance book into any system.

## First: does it track the asset, or only the account?

This is the fastest way to disqualify a tool. Most lending software records a loan beautifully and has no idea what secures it.

Vehicle finance needs both. Ask to see the **vehicle record** and confirm it holds:

- Registration number, make, model and year
- **Chassis and engine numbers**
- A photo of the RC book
- The **insurance expiry date**
- A link to the specific loan it secures

And one more thing that sounds minor until it isn't: can **one customer hold several vehicles**, each with its own loan? A customer with a goods tempo and two bikes is ordinary in this business, and a system that models one asset per borrower will have you creating duplicate customers inside a month.

The reason this matters operationally is simple. You cannot act on an overdue account until you know which asset backs it. And collateral sitting on **lapsed insurance** is worth materially less if it comes back to you damaged — an expiry date you are not tracking is a discount you will discover at the worst moment.

## Second: does it separate your charges from your interest?

Ask most vehicle finance owners where the profit comes from and they will say interest. Then look at a date-wise **profit and loss ledger** and the answer is usually more complicated.

On a book with frequent disbursement, the charges collected at origination — processing fee, documentation, whatever you call them — can rival or exceed the interest earned over the same period.

So check whether the ledger shows **each deduction charge in its own column**, and whether those columns are generated from **your own rule names** rather than forced into a fixed category list someone else designed. If every charge collapses into "other income", you have lost the ability to tell whether a slow month was a lending problem or a recovery problem.

## Third — and this is the real test — what happens when a seized vehicle is sold?

Ask the vendor this exact question and listen carefully.

A surprising number of systems post the sale proceeds as a **payment against the loan**. It is tempting: the outstanding clears, the account closes cleanly, the ledger balances. It is also the single most distorting thing a vehicle finance system can do.

Booking ₹85,000 of sale proceeds as a "collection":

- **Inflates your collection figures** for a month in which recovery actually failed
- **Credits a field officer** with a collection they did not make
- **Hides the failure** — the account that had to be seized stops looking like a problem

> A financier whose software merges collections and seizure proceeds genuinely cannot tell a strong collection month from a month of heavy seizures.

The right behaviour is that a repossession sale **never** appears as a collection or a payment — not against the loan, not on the dashboard, not in the daily collection report, and not against anyone's performance. Selling collateral is the liquidation of an asset you were holding, not cash collected from a borrower.

## Fourth: can it tell you whether a repossession actually earned anything?

Keeping the two apart is only half the job. The other half is measuring the seizure on its own terms. Look for a **recovery P&L** that carries:

1. **Valuation at seizure**, frozen at the moment you took the vehicle so a historical record doesn't drift when market prices move
2. **Outstanding on the loan** at that same moment
3. **Refurbishment cost** — what you spent making it sellable
4. **Sale amount**, and how any surplus splits between your share and a payout owed back to the customer
5. **Days held**, from seizure to sale, because a vehicle in your yard for ninety days carries a cost
6. **Shortfall** — the dues the sale did not cover

Two checks here separate serious software from a spreadsheet with a login.

Can the **net figure go negative**, and does the system report it as a loss? A vehicle that cost more to store and refurbish than its surplus returned is not a recovery, however much the sale amount flatters the story. Most owners think of repossession as getting their money back; an honest register frequently shows it as an expensive way to lose less.

And is **shortfall tracked rather than hidden**? When the sale doesn't cover the dues, that gap must stay visible on the record instead of disappearing into a closed loan. A book that quietly forgets its shortfalls will overstate how well seizures are working, every single time.

## Fifth: the ordinary field-collection questions

Once the vehicle-specific checks pass, the rest is the same checklist as any lending book — and worth running properly, because this is where the daily hours go:

| Check | Why it matters in auto finance |
|---|---|
| **EMI schedules**, with foreclosure and pre-closure | Vehicle loans are EMI loans; early closure is common and shouldn't be worked out on paper |
| **Offline capture** | Collections happen at stands, shops and homes on patchy networks |
| **GPS and photo proof** per visit | "I went, they weren't home" is unverifiable otherwise |
| **DPD ageing** | So follow-ups are ranked by recoverability, not by who called last |
| **Early discount and late fine** recorded as their own amounts | A waiver that silently adjusts the balance looks like money never owed to you |
| **Data ownership** | Borrower KYC plus asset records deserves a database you control |

Speed of entry belongs on this list too. Officers under-report when recording is slow, which is why [voice entry](/voice-entry-collection-app) matters more than it sounds — the agent speaks the amount instead of typing on a cramped form in the sun.
